\subsection{Radio-only}

The 144 MHz radio images contain information beyond the realistic baselines considered here.
On the 352 clusters imaged at snapshot-99, the CNN that pooled all three orthogonal projections achieved $R^2 = 0.485 \pm 0.028$, increasing to $0.530$ when predictions were averaged across five seeds.
This is compared to $R^2 = 0.314$ from ridge regression on the halo mass, 0.440 using integrated radio emission, and 0.459 using all five scalar features.
Combining the scalar features with the CNN gave $R^2 = 0.541$, an increment of 0.082 over the scalar model.

The CNN remained predictive within each mass tercile, with $R^2$ values of 0.332, 0.328, and 0.239 in the lowest, medium, and highest mass bins, respectively.
In comparison, the five-scalar model achieved $R^2=$ 0.295, 0.250, and $-0.287$.
The partial rank correlation between pseudo-TSC and the CNN predictions after controlling for halo mass was 0.480.
Thus, as the CNN achieved greater $R^2$ for each tercile, its performance is not explained solely by the overall association between mass and pseudo-TSC.
However, the scalar features predicted the CNN prediction with $R^2=0.732$, and thus they have substantial overlap.

Normalizing each radio projection to a common integrated emission value reduced the mean CNN score to $R^2=0.414 \pm 0.040$.
Performance decreased in all five random seeds, with a mean paired change of $-0.071$ and a paired-seed p-value of $0.019$.
Therefore, explicit amplitude information is helpful for prediction, but not the only component.
Note that amplitude normalization does not isolate morphology specifically from all information related to luminosity: the original integrated emission predicted the output of the normalized-map model at $R^2=0.602$.
Thus, morphology, brightness, and cluster properties remain correlated within the simulated population.

Part of the signal could also be recovered from a representation of the morphology.
An XGBoost decision tree model applied to 48 Minkowski-functional measurements predicted pseudo-TSC at $R^2 = 0.304$ for individual projections, and at $R^2 = 0.415$ after averaging predictions across all three cluster projections.
At the largest area-fraction threshold, corresponding to the lowest intensity threshold considered \todo{this phrasing is unclear}, the number of connected components correlated with pseudo-TSC at Spearman correlation $\rho = 0.62$.
Older proxy ages were thus associated with more fragmented emission.
A similar analysis applied to clean maps at 1.4 GHz gave similar results, with scores differing by no more than 0.02.

\subsection{X-ray and Combined Model}

Clean X-ray images provided comparable predictive power to clean radio images.
Applying the globally-scaled arcsinh scaling increased the X-ray CNN correlation from $R^2=0.320$ to $0.511 \pm 0.26$.
Improvement was found in all five seeds, with a paired-seed p-value of $0.0003$.
This shows a large sensitivity to the preprocessing method, reflecting the the limited compression produced by applying an unscaled arcsinh transformation to low count rates.
\todo{this isn't explained very well}
Combining the stretched X-ray images with the 144 MHz radio maps yields a mean $R^2 = 0.545$.
The paired increment over just radio was $0.059 \pm 0.025$, with all five seeds showing improvement ($p = 0.007$).
This result supports the existence of complementary information between X-ray and radio data.
However, note that the quoted significance describes variation across training seeds, and not uncertainty across independently-sampled halo populations.

\subsection{With Forward-Modelling}
\label{sec:flux}

Post-processing the radio images with a forward model substantially reduced the information that could be extracted.
Results in table \ref{tab:realistic} are obtained with radio emission cropped and rebinned in linear units with flux conservation, while X-ray photon sampling follows the exposure conventions of the processed observations.
These models were trained on snapshots 99 and 91 and evaluated on all 352 clusters at snapshot 99.
We distinguish the pooled-view score, which combines all three projections, from the single-view score, which evaluates each projection separately after averaging the five model predictions.

For single projections, the radio-only ensemble of five models achieved $R^2 = 0.072$ with a box size of 1 Mpc, and $R^2=0.203$ with a box size of 3 Mpc.
The corresponding scores for the pooled input models, averaged over the different seeds, were $0.142\pm0.021$ and $0.238\pm0.019$.
The experiment sensitive to arcsinh morphology \todo{explain better}, which retained the total physical flux while compressing the spatial intensity distribution, gave a similar single-projection score of $R^2=0.207$ for the 3 Mpc box.
While the models predicting on larger boxes performed better, injection placements were also changed, so the effect of field size was not completely isolated.

Despite significantly decreased performance, the radio images retained useful information beyond noisy mass measurements.
The mass-only model achieved a single-observation ensemble score of $R^2 = 0.207$, which increased to $0.306$ when the 3 Mpc radio image was also provided.
The mean paired difference was $+0.099$, with a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[+0.039, +0.158]$.
Both experiments used the same fixed noisy mass measurements, but the specific fold partitions differed; thus, the confidence interval describes the contrast between the fitted models and the partitions, rather than a fully-matched radio contribution estimate.

X-ray images showed much stronger performance under forward-modelling, achieving a single-observation ensemble $R^2 = 0.406$.
Adding the radio images with 1 Mpc box size increased the score to $0.436$, a mean difference of $+0.031$.
However, this difference was not significant, with a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[-0.014, +0.076]$.
This comparison also used different fold partitions.

A separate experiment tested whether training on individual X-ray views improved performance when inference was restricted to a single observation.
Both conditions used identical folds and single-view inner validation scoring.
Pooled-observation training achieved an ensemble $R^2 = 0.425$ and mean absolute error (MAE) of $0.649$ Gyr, compared with $R^2=0.421$ and MAE of $0.659$ Gyr for single-view training.
The paired $R^2$ difference was $-0.004$, with a $95\%$ confidence interval of $[-0.027, +0.019]$.
Therefore, single-view training showed no ensemble improvement in this experiment.
These results create a separate comparison from those in table \ref{tab:realistic}, since the fold construction and checkpoint selection protocol differ \todo{how?}.

The performance on X-ray data also depended on exposure.
In the depth experiment, the mean pooled-view $R^2$ increased from 0.312 at 5 ks to 0.365 at 20 ks and 0.450 at 50 ks.
Further increases were not as large, reaching 0.476 at 150 ks and 0.499 at 500 ks.
These exposures are expressed in Cycle-22-equivalent units.
This experiment used three seeds, and only trained on data from snapshot 99, so its scores are not directly interchangable with the single-observation, five-model results stated previously.
The trend in accuracy with exposure time describes sensitivity within the approximate count and background model; observation-specific detector footprints are not reproduced.

Figure \ref{fig:tsc_pred} shows that the recovered age range is systematically compressed for the forward-modelled data.
The youngest mergers are predicted about 0.35 Gyr too old, while systems at the 4 Gyr maximum were predicted about 1.2-1.4 Gyr below the maximum.
Thus, substantial bias exists near the limits of the label distribution, especially near the 4 Gyr maximum.

The simulated radio power also varied systematically with the merger state and the mass.
After applying the density cut, the total 144 MHz power had a fitted mass slope of $2.67\pm0.16$ and scatter of $0.78$ dex.
Restricting the emission to the 3 Mpc injection box resulted in a slope of $2.36\pm0.19$ and scatter of 0.93 dex.
Residuals from the total-power relation decreased with pseudo-TSC, with Spearman $\rho=-0.33$.
Thus, recently disturbed systems tend to be brighter at a fixed mass.
The normalization was set globally, anchored to the observed halo-power relation; the simulated slope was not imposed.
Comparisons with observed halo and relic relations remain contextual, as frequency, source definition, fitting method, and selection differ, and the slope alone does not establish agreement with one particular source population.

All statistical models trained on forward-modelled data used images from both snapshot 99 and 91.
A matched comparison of one- and two-epoch training has not been conducted, quantifying the benefit of an additional epoch is left to future work.

\subsection{Loss, output head and contrastive pretraining}
\label{sec:loss_head}

We also tested alternative losses and output representations in an exploratory X-ray experiment.
The Huber loss baseline achieved a five-seed single-projection ensemble $R^2 = 0.432$ and MAE of 0.650. Gyr.
Using the mean squared error loss instead gave $R^2 = 0.425$ and MAE of 0.663 Gyr.
The seven-class distributional head \todo{define} gave $R^2 = 0.425$ with MAE of 0.666 Gyr.
Their paired $R^2$ differences from the Huber loss case were $-0.005$, with $95\%$ confidence interval of $[-0.029, +0.021]$ and $-0.007$ with confidence interval $[-0.034, +0.025]$ respectively.
Neither alternative met the prespecified point-prediction adoption criterion \todo{define}.

However, the distributional head provided useful probabilistic predictions relative to the class frequencies among fitting halos.
Its categorical log score \todp{define everything in this paragraph} was 1.649 compared with 1.888 for the reference.
Its normalized ranked probability score was 0.125 compared with 0.160.
Its Brier score for the no-crossing category was 0.068 compared with 0.088.
All three paired conditional intervals favored the model.
These results establish probabilistic skill relative to the baseline model, but do not establish calibrated uncertainty in physical collision times.

A pilot study using contrastive pretraining showed that this method performed significantly worse than the Huber loss control.
Rotations and reflections were applied to individual observations to construct positive pairs, followed by an encoder with weights frozen during downstream training.
This yielded $R^2=0.232$ and MAE of 0.768 Gyr.
The paired changes were $-0.200$ in $R^2$, with confidence interval $[-0.260, -0.132]$ and change in MAE of $+0.118$ and interval $[+0.074, +0.162]$ Gyr.
This result provides evidence against using the tested frozen-encoder configuration, though other contrastive objectives, or fine-tuning the encoder, could change the performance.

\subsection{Idealised optical structure}
\label{sec:optical}

Idealized optical structure supplied predictive information in addition to the scalar control model.
Adding magnitude-gap and positional-structure features to the noisy mass, placement redshift, and projected richness \todo{define} increased the single-projection ensemble $R^2$ from 0.313 to 0.422 and reduced MAE from 0.729 to 0.655 Gyr.
The paired $R^2$ increase was $0.109$, with $95\%$ confidence interval of $[+0.048, +0.167]$.
In the same experiment, adding X-ray images to the controls increased $R^2$ to 0.488, an increment of $+0.176$ with confidence interval $[+0.108, +0.243]$ and MAE of 0.605 Gyr.

However, adding the optical structure features to the X-ray images and controls did not demonstrate a further improvement, with $R^2=0.469$ and MAE of 0.632 Gyr, and paired $R^2$ change of $-0.019$ with confidence interval $[-0.067, +0.029]$.
These features use simulation-defined membership, true $R_{500}$, and an idealized hot gas reference center \todo{define}.
Thus, this experiment measures performance only with idealized combined inputs, rather than performance achievable from true optical catalogues.

\subsection{Application to LoVoCCS}

Finally, we applied our predictive models to LoVoCCS clusters, obtaining radio predictions for 26 systems, and X-ray predictions for 15.
All 15 X-ray systems were present in the radio sample, and thus could use combined data.
Each observed prediction averaged 25 models spanning five folds and five seeds.
Among the systems with both data sources, radio and X-ray predictions had Spearman $\rho = 0.61$ ($p=0.015$).
After controlling for redshift, the partial-rank correlation was 0.71, with a Freedman-Lane \todo{define} permutation $p=0.005$.
Controlling instead for weak-lensing mass gave a partial correlation of 0.54 among the 11 systems with available masses, with $p=0.11$.
This latter comparison does not establish agreement independent of mass.

Despite the correlated rankings between the predictions derived from radio and X-ray data, the two sources systematically disagreed in their absolute predictions.
The radio model assigned older proxy ages than the X-ray model to 14 of the 15 clusters with both sources, with a median paired offset of 0.44 Gyr.
Their respective median predictions were 1.10 and and 0.83 Gyr.
Applying the forward-model did not remove this offset, and its origin remains unidentified.
