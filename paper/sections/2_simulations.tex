\section{Simulations and labels}
\label{sec:sims}

\subsection{TNG-Cluster}

We use the TNG-Cluster suite of cosmological magnetohydrodynamical zoom simulations \citep{Nelson2024, Nelson2019}. The suite comprises 352 massive
galaxy clusters, spanning
$M_{200\mathrm{c}} \simeq 10^{14.3}$--$10^{15.4}\,\mathrm{M_\odot}$ at redshift 0.

TNG-Cluster adopts a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology consistent with the Planck 2015 results \citep{Planck2016}:
$\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.3089$,
$\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.6911$,
$\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}=0.0486$, and
$h=0.6774$, corresponding to
$H_0=67.74\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$.

For this work, we use the 352 primary clusters at snapshot-99 ($z=0$), and 351 of their corresponding progenitors at snapshot-91 $z = 0.0994$.
The two snapshots are separated by 1.336 Gyr.
The median snapshot-99 minus snapshot-91 mass difference is $0.029$ dex, and the matched pseudo-TSC values have Spearman correlation $\rho=0.324$.
We construct three orthogonal projections for each cluster at each snapshot to generate the synthetic radio and X-ray observations.
Since different projections and snapshots of the same cluster are not independent, all data corresponding to individual clusters are kept in the same fold during training.

Each map is centred on the FoF group position (\texttt{GroupPos}), and
we use the corresponding $R_{500\mathrm{c}}$ and
$M_{500\mathrm{c}}$ values at each snapshot.

\subsection{Shock-powered radio emission}
\label{sec:shock_radio}

We model shock-powered synchrotron emission using the electron
acceleration and cooling prescription of \citet{HoeftBruggen2007},
expressing the electron energy budget in terms of the shock
dissipation rate as in \citet{Lee2024}. The quantities entering the
calculation are the Mach number $\mathcal{M}$, magnetic-field
strength $B$, gas temperature $T$ which is calculated from internal energy, and energy dissipation rate $\dot{E}_{\rm diss}$ of each
shock cell in TNG-Cluster, converted to physical units. We select
cells with $\mathcal{M}>1.3$ and $\dot{E}_{\rm diss}>0$. The Mach number is directly read from the snapshots of TNG-Cluster. The shock finder for the post-processing is briefly introduced in \citet{Lee2024}, and the reader can find more details in \citet{Schaal2015}.

For a gas adiabatic index $\gamma_{\rm g}=5/3$, the compression
ratio $r$ and the injected electron spectral index $s$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r(\mathcal{M}) &=
\frac{(\gamma_{\rm g}+1)\mathcal{M}^{2}}
     {(\gamma_{\rm g}-1)\mathcal{M}^{2}+2} = \frac{4\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mathcal{M}^{2}+3},\\
s(\mathcal{M}) &= \frac{r+2}{r-1}
=\frac{2(\mathcal{M}^{2}+1)}{\mathcal{M}^{2}-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radio_shock_slope}
\end{equation}
Let $E$ be the electron kinetic energy and define
$\tilde e=E/(m_{\rm e}c^{2})$. Following equation~(14) of
\citet{HoeftBruggen2007}, we adopt the injected spectrum
\begin{equation}
n_E(E,0)=
\frac{n_{\rm e}C_{\rm spec}}{m_{\rm e}c^{2}}\,
\tilde e^{-s}
\left(1-\frac{\tilde e}{\tilde e_{\max}}\right)^{s-2},
\label{eq:radio_injected_spectrum}
\end{equation}
for $\tilde e_{\min}\leq\tilde e<\tilde e_{\max}$, and zero
otherwise. The quantity $n_E\,dE$ is the number density of
accelerated electrons in the interval $[E,E+dE]$;
$n_{\rm e}$ is the thermal electron number density and
$C_{\rm spec}$ fixes the normalization. We use
\begin{equation}
\tilde e_{\min}=\frac{10k_{\rm B}T_{\rm d}}{m_{\rm e}c^{2}},
\qquad
\tilde e_{\max}=10^{7},
\label{eq:radio_energy_cutoffs}
\end{equation}
taking the local temperature of the selected shock cell as an
estimate of the downstream temperature $T_{\rm d}$.
The lower cutoff approximates the thermal--nonthermal matching
prescription of \citet{HoeftBruggen2007}. 

For a steady shock patch of area $A$ and downstream speed $v_{\rm d}$
in the shock frame, a fraction $\xi_{\rm e}$ of the dissipated power
is supplied to accelerated electrons:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\xi_{\rm e}\dot{E}_{\rm diss}
&= A v_{\rm d}\int_{E_{\min}}^{E_{\max}}
E\,n_E(E,0)\,dE\\
&= A v_{\rm d}n_{\rm e}C_{\rm spec}m_{\rm e}c^{2}
I_{\rm spec}(s,T_{\rm d}),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radio_energy_budget}
\end{equation}
as in equation~(6) of \citet{Lee2024}. We retain the
\citet{HoeftBruggen2007} notation
\begin{equation}
I_{\rm spec}(s,T_{\rm d})=
\int_{\tilde e_{\min}(T_{\rm d})}^{\tilde e_{\max}}
\tilde e^{\,1-s}
\left(1-\frac{\tilde e}{\tilde e_{\max}}\right)^{s-2}
d\tilde e .
\label{eq:radio_ispec}
\end{equation}
We adopt $\xi_{\rm e}=0.05$.

Then the high energy electrons are cooled by Inverse Compton Scattering with CMB photons ad the synchrotron loss, we ignore the adiabatic cooling just as \citet{HoeftBruggen2007}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\tilde e}{dt}&=-C_{\rm cool}\tilde e^{2},\\
C_{\rm cool}&=
\frac{\sigma_{\rm T}}{6\pi m_{\rm e}c}
\left(B^{2}+B_{\rm CMB}^{2}\right),\\
B_{\rm CMB}&=3.24(1+z)^{2}\,\mu{\rm G},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radio_cooling}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\rm T}$ is the Thomson cross-section and $z$ is
the simulation redshift.
We use the radiative-cooling solution of \citet{Kardashev1962}, also adopted by
\citet{HoeftBruggen2007}. Using the notation of the latter, the cooled electron distribution and its integral over electron age, for $E_{\min}\leq E<E_{\max}$, are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
n_E(E,t)
&= \frac{n_{\rm e}C_{\rm spec}}{m_{\rm e}c^2}
\,\tilde e^{-s}
\quad\times
\left[
1-\left(
\frac{1}{\tilde e_{\max}}+C_{\rm cool}t
\right)\tilde e
\right]^{s-2},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radio_cooled_spectrum}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^\infty n_E(E,t)\,\mathrm{d}t
&= \frac{n_{\rm e}C_{\rm spec}}
{m_{\rm e}c^2 C_{\rm cool}(s-1)}
\,\tilde e^{-s-1}
\quad\times
\left(
1-\frac{\tilde e}{\tilde e_{\max}}
\right)^{s-1}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radio_age_integral}
\end{equation}
The first expression applies for $0\leq t<t_{\max}(E)$,
where
$t_{\max}(E)=(\tilde e^{-1}-\tilde e_{\max}^{-1})/C_{\rm cool}$.
For $t\geq t_{\max}(E)$, $n_E(E,t)=0$.

We use Gaussian cgs units for the radiation coefficients.
Following the approximate pitch-angle treatment of
\citet{HoeftBruggen2007}, we replace $\sin\alpha$ by its isotropic
mean $\pi/4$ in the single-electron spectrum and characteristic
frequency. The spectral power is then
\begin{equation}
P_e(\nu,E)
=
C_{\rm sync}
F\!\left(\frac{\nu}{\nu_c}\right).
\label{eq:radio_single_electron}
\end{equation}
The normalization and characteristic frequency are
\begin{equation}
C_{\rm sync}
=
\frac{\sqrt{3}\,\pi e^3 B}{4m_{\rm e}c^2},
\label{eq:radio_csync}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\nu_c
=
\frac{3eB}{16m_{\rm e}c}\,
\gamma_{\rm L}^{\,2}.
\label{eq:radio_nuc}
\end{equation}
The synchrotron kernel is defined as
\begin{equation}
F(x)
=
x\int_x^\infty K_{5/3}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t.
\label{eq:radio_kernel}
\end{equation}
Here $e$ is the magnitude of the electron charge,
$K_{5/3}$ is the modified Bessel function of the second kind,
and $\gamma_{\rm L}=1+\tilde e\simeq\tilde e$ for the
radio-emitting electrons. The frequency $\nu$ is measured in
the source rest frame.

The spectral luminosity of the patch, $L_\nu=dP/d\nu$, is
obtained by integrating over the downstream distance
$y=v_{\rm d}t$:
\begin{equation}
L_\nu\simeq A v_{\rm d}
\int_{E_{\min}}^{E_{\max}} dE\,P_{\rm e}(\nu,E)
\int_0^\infty dt\,n_E(E,t).
\label{eq:radio_downstream_integral}
\end{equation}
We assume that the electrons dominating the radio emission satisfy
$\tilde e_{\min}\ll\tilde e_{\rm radio}\ll\tilde e_{\max}$.
The cutoff factor in equation~\eqref{eq:radio_age_integral}
can then be neglected in the radiation integral, and its energy
limits extended to zero and infinity. This approximation is
applied to the radiation integral only; Eq.~\eqref{eq:radio_ispec}
retains both finite cutoffs.

Introducing the dimensionless variables
\begin{equation}
C_\tau=\left(\frac{3eB}{16m_{\rm e}c\nu}\right)^{1/2},
\qquad \tau=C_\tau\tilde e ,
\label{eq:radio_ctau}
\end{equation}
we obtain $\nu/\nu_{\rm c}\simeq\tau^{-2}$. The remaining
radiation integral is the $I_\Psi$ of equation~(31) of
\citet{HoeftBruggen2007}, after integration over age:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
I_\Psi(s)
&=\frac{1}{s-1}\int_0^\infty
\tau^{-s-1}F(\tau^{-2})\,d\tau\\
&=\frac{1}{(s-1)(s+2)}
\int_0^\infty t^{s/2+1}K_{5/3}(t)\,dt\\
&=\frac{2^{s/2}}{(s-1)(s+2)}
\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s}{4}+\frac{11}{6}\right)
\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s}{4}+\frac{1}{6}\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radio_ipsi}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is the Euler gamma function. The final equality follows from the standard integral identity for the modified Bessel function
\citep[Eq.~10.43.19]{olver_nist_2010}.

Combining equations~\eqref{eq:radio_energy_budget} and
\eqref{eq:radio_downstream_integral} gives
\begin{equation}
L_\nu\simeq
\frac{\xi_{\rm e}\dot{E}_{\rm diss}}{m_{\rm e}c^{2}}\,
\frac{C_{\rm sync}}{C_{\rm cool}}\,
C_\tau^{\,s}\,
\frac{I_\Psi(s)}{I_{\rm spec}(s,T_{\rm d})}.
\label{eq:radio_luminosity_physical}
\end{equation}
The shock area, downstream speed, and electron-density
normalization have thus been eliminated in favour of the
dissipated power.

For direct evaluation at a reference frequency of $144\,{\rm MHz}$,
define $b=B/(1\,\mu{\rm G})$ and
$b_{\rm CMB}=B_{\rm CMB}/(1\,\mu{\rm G})$.
Substituting the physical constants into
equation~\eqref{eq:radio_luminosity_physical} yields
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
L_\nu\simeq{}&
\left(8.70125\times10^{34}\,
{\rm W\,Hz^{-1}}\right)
\left(\frac{\xi_{\rm e}}{0.05}\right)
\left(\frac{\dot{E}_{\rm diss}}
{10^{44}\,{\rm erg\,s^{-1}}}\right)\\
&\times
\left(2.29013\times10^{-8}\right)^{s/2}
\frac{I_\Psi(s)}{I_{\rm spec}(s,T_{\rm d})}\\
&\times
\frac{b^{\,1+s/2}}{b^{2}+b_{\rm CMB}^{2}}
\left(\frac{\nu}{144\,{\rm MHz}}\right)^{-s/2}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:radio_luminosity_144}
\end{equation}
The dimensionless base in the second line is
$3eB_{\rm ref}/(16m_{\rm e}c\nu_{\rm ref})$, evaluated at
$B_{\rm ref}=1\,\mu{\rm G}$ and
$\nu_{\rm ref}=144\,{\rm MHz}$.
The numerical normalization is calculated from the physical
constants and the adopted injection and pitch-angle
prescriptions. The explicit temperature dependence enters through
$\tilde e_{\min}(T_{\rm d})$ in $I_{\rm spec}$.

The 144 MHz images created from this emission model have a substantial concentration of emission in dense gas, with approximately 99\% of radio weight in gas with $n_\text{H} > 10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$.
Dense gas emission is inconsistent with the assumptions of the shock emission model.
Thus, we apply a density cut to restrict the model to its applicable domain, eliminating emission from gas with $n_\text{H} > 10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$.
The median fraction of a cluster's emission located in its brightest pixel falls from 25\% to 5.1\% after applying the density cut.
The corresponding radio-weighted mean Mach-number falls from 61.7 to 4.8.

The frequency factor is applied separately to each shock cell, since its spectral index is dependent on Mach number.
After dividing the 144 MHz weights by a global factor of 12.5, the logarithmic ratio to the 1.4 GHz fluxes has standard deviation 0.035 dex.
Approximately 5\% of the normalized flux is redistributed between pixels.

\subsection{X-ray mocks}

We generated synthetic X-ray observations using the package \textsc{pyXSIM} \citep{ZuHone2016} and SOXS \citep{ZuHone2023}, with an Astrophysical Plasma Emission Code (APEC) set to be in collisional ionization equilibrium.
The emitting gas was selected within a cube of side length $2 R_{500\text{c}}$ centered on the FoF position, using the halo properties at the selected snapshot.
We retain gas with $T > 3 \times 10^5$ K, zero star formation rate, and \texttt{GFM\_CoolingRate} $<0$.
The source model uses the simulated gas temperatures, metallicities, and emission measures.

For each cluster, photons were projected along each of three orthogonal directions corresponding to the three radio projections.
The snapshot-99 clusters are placed at an observing redshift of $z_{\text{obs}} = 0.05$, while the snapshot-91 clusters are observed at $z_\text{obs} = 0.0994$.
We use photoelectric absorption using the \texttt{wabs} model, with a uniform hydrogen column density of $N_\text{H}= 10^{20}$ cm$^{-2}$.
The photons were then processed using the \textsc{SOXS} Chandra ACIS-I Cycle-22 instrument model, using an exposure time of 2 Ms.
These observations take into account the instrument response, point-spread function, detector geometry, dithering, and instrumental particle background.
Galactic foreground and point-source backgrounds were disabled when generating these initial mocks, and were added during the forward modelling in section \ref{sec:fwd}.

We constructed exposure-corrected images in the observed 0.1--2.0 keV band using exposure maps evaluated at 1.2 keV.
The native images have a pixel scale of 0.492".

% For snapshot-99, we crop the images to $128\times 128$ pixels, which corresponds to approximately 18.9 kpc per pixel.
% The ACIS-I field has a radius of approximately 1 Mpc at $z_\text{obs} = 0.05$, so the usable coverage is set by the detector, rather than the available image data.

For snapshot-99, we crop the native 4880$\times$4880 images to 4864$\times$4864 pixels, then average the signal over $38\times38$ pixel blocks, obtaining a $128\times128$ pixel grid with physical pixel size approximately 18.9 kpc.
The full image grid extends beyond the exposed detector footprint, which has half-width of approximately 500 kpc at $z_\text{obs} = 0.05$.
At the snapshot-91 observing redshift, $z_\text{obs} = 0.0994$, the detector covers a larger physical radius.

In the subsequent forward-modelling step, we recover integer count estimates from the exposure-corrected images.
The snapshot-91 images were placed on the same physical grid as snapshot-99 by binomially allocating each native pixel's counts between overlapping output pixels in proportion to their overlap areas.
This procedure preserves integer counts, although their recovery is approximate where the original exposure varies across chip gaps and dither edges.

Before training, we compress the image dynamic range using $\operatorname{arcsinh}(x/a)$, where $x$ is the image signal and $a$ is a single scale factor used for each image from both snapshots.
The adjustments to target redshift, exposure, luminosity, absorption, and sky background are described in section \ref{sec:xray}.

\subsection{Merger-time labels}
% We characterize the merger state of each target halo using the time since collision (TSC). Using the major merger catalog of \cite{Lee2024}, we identify the major merger whose first pericenter passage is closest in time to the snapshot of interest. We then define the TSC label as
% \begin{equation}
% {\rm TSC} = t_{\rm snap} - t_{\rm merger},
% \end{equation}
% where $t_{\rm snap}$ is the cosmic time of the target snapshot and $t_{\rm merger}$ is the time of the first pericenter passage of the nearest merger.

Our primary regression target is the \emph{pseudo-TSC}, a proxy for recent merger activity constructed from the precomputed TNG-Cluster merger scores.
For each snapshot and halo, the merger score was sampled with window parameters $\tau = 0.1, 0.2, \ldots 4.0$ Gyr.
The pseudo-TSC is defined as the value of $\tau$ at which the merger score first reaches 0.5, using linear interpolation between the two samples which bracket the threshold value:
\begin{equation}
    T_\text{pseudo}
    = \tau_{j-1} + 
     \frac{0.5-S(\tau_{j-1})}
       {S(\tau_j)-S(\tau_{j-1})}
  (\tau_j-\tau_{j-1}),
\end{equation}
where $j$ is the first sampled window with $S(\tau_j) \geq 0.5$ and $S(\tau_i)$ is the merger score at $\tau_i$, defined as \citep{KongDellAntonio2026}
\begin{equation}
    S(\tau_j)
    = \sum_\text{mergers} \exp \left(-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau_j} \right)
\end{equation}
in which $\Delta t$ is the time between a merger event and the fixed snapshot being labelled, and $j$ indexes the sampled decay timescales $\tau_j$.
If the merger score is already at the 0.5 threshold at the smallest window, we assign $T_\text{pseudo} = 0.1$ Gyr.
If there is no crossing within the sampled range, we assign $T_\text{pseudo} = 4$ Gyr.
For snapshot-91, we used the past-only merger score, excluding contributions from collisions that occurred after that snapshot.
At snapshot-99, the past-only and all-event scores are identical, since the recorded history ends at that snapshot.
Using the all-event score for snapshot-91 images would change the interpolated labels for 168 of the 351 halos.

For comparison, we also consider a collision score constructed from the merger catalogue of \citet{Lee2024}, measuring the elapsed cosmic time since the most recent first-pericenter collision at or before the selected snapshot:
\begin{equation}
  T_{\rm cat} =
  t_{\rm snap}
  - \max_{k:\,t_{{\rm coll},k}\leq t_{\rm snap}}
  t_{{\rm coll},k}.
\end{equation}
In the comparison labels, we obtain collision times by converting the recorded collision snapshots to cosmic time.
We impose no additional mass-ratio cut within the selection limits of the catalogue.
At snapshot-99, the events defining $T_\text{cat}$ have median secondary-to-primary mass ratio of 0.093 (approximately 1:11), so $T_\text{cat}$ predominantly measures minor collisions rather than major mergers.

Figure~\ref{fig:labels} shows the pseudo-TSC distributions and their correlation to $T_\text{cat}$.
The median pseudo-TSC is 0.92 Gyr for the 352 snapshot-99 clusters, and 0.91 Gyr for the 351 snapshot-91 clusters.
Respectively, 34 and 47 objects reach the 4 Gyr dynamic range cap, corresponding to 9.7\% and 13.4\% of samples.
At snapshot-99, pseudo-TSC and $T_\text{cat}$  have a Spearman rank correlation of 0.961 across the 346 clusters with finite catalogue times.
The six remaining clusters have no recorded collision and are assigned capped pseudo-TSC labels.

Pseudo-TSC measures a threshold-crossing window scale, rather than the elapsed time since an individually-identified pericentric passage.
Its rank correlation with $T_\text{cat}$ supports its use as an indicator of recent merger activity, but does not calibrate errors in the physical collision age.
The cap at 4 Gyr prevents resolution of merger histories beyond the sampled window.
