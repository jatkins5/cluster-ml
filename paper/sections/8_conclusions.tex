\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We investigated the constraining power of radio and X-ray images on recent galaxy cluster merger activity using convolutional neural networks trained on mocks constructed from TNG-Cluster snapshots.
We constructed simulated images and observational forward models for comparison with LOFAR and Chandra data, and applied the trained networks to nearby clusters from the LoVoCCS sample.
Our prediction target, pseudo-TSC, provides a continuous measure of recent merger activity, and correlates strongly with the time since the most recent collision exposed by the TNG-Cluster catalogue.
However, its interpretation depends on the merger score definition and the 4 Gyr label cap.

The CNN was able to recover information about the merger time from both radio and X-ray images.
For clean images with all three pooled projections per cluster, the networks achieved mean out-of-fold $R^2$ values of 0.485 for radio, 0.511 for X-ray, and 0.545 for the combined inputs.
The radio network remained predictive within mass bins, and after normalizing the mock images to a common integrated emission value, indicating that morphology contains information in addition to luminosity.
However, correlations between morphology, mass, and luminosity prevent a complete analysis of their individual contributions.
The improved performance of the combined radio and X-ray model indicates the potential value of utilizing both bands under these imaging conditions.

The details of construction of the simulated observations are consequential for their physical interpretation and applicability.
Before applying an emitting-gas density cut, a median of 99\% of the model radio weight originated in gas denser than $n_\text{H} = 10^{-4}$ cm$^{-3}$, including in cold gas outside of the diffuse intracluster medium targeted by the emission model.
Restricting the emitting gas reduced this concentration.
This result demonstrates the need to examine the gas phases that dominate the synthetic emission maps when constructing simulated images.

Under our observational forward model, X-ray images retained stronger predictive performance of the pseudo-TSC than radio images.
For individual simulated observations, five-model ensembles achieved $R^2 = 0.406$ for X-ray and $R^2=0.203$ for radio images with 3 Mpc physical side lengths.
An exploratory comparison suggested that radio images do add information beyond a noisy mass measurement, although this result requires confirmation using matched cross-validation partitions.
Combining the 1 Mpc radio images with X-ray images yielded no statistically significant improvement.
This, the complementary information between radio and X-ray data present in the clean images appeared to be eroded by realistic observing conditions for these apertures.

The forward-modelled 1 Mpc joint configuration demonstrated no performance improvement over X-ray inputs alone; however, a matched comparison using the 3 Mpc radio aperture is still needed.
A separate exposure experiment also showed improved X-ray performance with increasing depth, with diminishing gains beyond approximately 50 ks.

Applying our trained networks to clusters in the LoVoCCS sample yielded radio predictions for 26 clusters and X-ray predictions for 15 clusters.
For the sample with both radio and X-ray data, the predictions had age ranks correlated at Spearman $\rho=0.61$, but radio assigned older proxy ages to 14 of the 15 systems, with a median offset of 0.44 Gyr.
The ordering agreement shows consistency between the predictive models, and therefore suggests some level of accuracy, but shared biases could also produce agreement.
Independent constraints on merger histories are needed to establish their accuracy on real clusters.
The offset in their predictions identifies an unresolved difference between methods.

These results establish the feasibility of recovering a continuous indicator of recent merger activity from simulated cluster observations, including images subjected to approximate survey observational degradation.
The principal challenges for estimating merger timing are now to establish the robustness of the emission models, calibrate predictions against independent constraints, and improve the matching of simulated and observed samples.
Validation must also address the compression of the predicted age range, as well as uncertainty beyond variation between training runs.
With these developments, inference from cluster images could support systematic analyses of connections between intracluster gas structure, merger history, and radio emission across large cluster populations.
